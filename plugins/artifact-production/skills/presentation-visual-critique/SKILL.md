# Presentation visual critique

## When to use

- Critique a presentation before or after rendering when it needs stronger hierarchy, visual rhythm, or relationship clarity.

## Constraints

- Do not write project files, generated artifacts, review verdicts, or releases.

This adviser is read-only. Start with the complete slide montage before inspecting individual pages. Check whether the cover speaks to the audience without exposing internal planning labels such as who the deck is "for". Compare the visible cover language with the private audience brief and flag unexplained explicit addressing.

Check nine v6 quality dimensions:

1. **Audience fit** — distinguish boundary from coverage. Reject internal audience labels on the cover, then check whether the visible deck answers the material decision needs of every role in a compound audience brief.
2. **Headline voice** — read only the title chain first. Classify each title as plain, specific, or formulaic. Compactness is only a constraint: repeated balanced clauses, abstract noun slogans, and mechanically parallel frames still fail when they make the deck sound generated. Labels, findings, and questions are all valid when the page earns them.
3. **Typography rhythm** — inspect baselines and the vertical position of text inside boxes, not just nominal font sizes. Compare visible line spacing, paragraph spacing, left/top alignment, CJK/Latin fit, automatic wraps, orphaned final lines, and clipping with the OOXML text-rhythm evidence. Flag dense text that appears vertically centered or compressed.
4. **Content encoding** — identify what the visual visibly communicates beyond the subtitle. Diagrams, data, screenshots, comparisons, or spatial structure should carry the main information; decorative boxes, badges, and takeaway strips must not merely restate prose. Do not use a storyboard label or intended assertion as evidence of the rendered payload.
5. **Grouping semantics** — peers are visibly grouped through real bullets, numbering, aligned stacks, or grids. Flag centered newline lists and irregular alignment that makes categories look unrelated.
6. **Layout rhythm** — repeated frames are intentional. Flag runs of interchangeable title-plus-cards-plus-footer layouts, especially when headline, composition, or similar-page signals recur. A blocking deck signal requires a source change, not a rationale.
7. **Relationship semantics** — compare the claim with the visible graph: a closed loop has a return path, a branch visibly forks or merges, a hierarchy has a root, and a cross-product claim has every matrix cell. Then verify physical reading direction, arrowheads, endpoints, crossings, associations, and break markers. A locally correct arrow can still produce a globally wrong reading path.
8. **Comparison truth** — options use the same criteria, evidence basis, and level of abstraction. Flag missing cells, selective positives, straw-man alternatives, and metrics without numeric values or sources.
9. **Access and distance** — check contrast, reading order, non-color encoding, and image quality. Judge at roughly 480×270; a screenshot whose fine text carries the argument needs a readable crop, callout, or summary.

Anchor every concern to a page and an observable feature. Separate deterministic probe failures from aesthetic judgment; do not claim that a schema or layout fingerprint proves audience comprehension.
